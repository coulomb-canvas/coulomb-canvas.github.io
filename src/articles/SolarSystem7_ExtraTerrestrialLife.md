Life is very hard to define, and can be controversial. But the requirements are better understood, especially through the study of extemophiles, mostly bacteria.

Some forms of life can survive

- Between \\( -20 \\) and \\( 121ºC \\) (like hydrothermal vents at mid ocean ridges)
- Ionising radiation 1000x the amount which would kill a human
- Chemical extremes, like 0.06-11 pH
- Pressure above 1100 bar

### Fundamental Requirements

1. An energy source, e.g. photosynthesis, chemosynthesis
2. Carbon source, valency allows complex organic chemistry. Unlikely to be the limiting factor, since carbon is a very common element
3. Liquid Water, required as a solvent of all life on earth. This is a limiting factor, since it's quite rare in liquid form

> In order for life to form, we need an energy source, a carbon source (for complex organic chemistry) and *liquid* water as a solvent

Carbon based life in water may not be the only possibility, but Hydrogen, Oxygen and Carbon are the 1st, 3rd and 6th most common elements in the universe, so there should be *some* carbon based life out there. 

Liquid water needs moderate temperatures and pressure greater than 0.006 bar. There is probably also a higher temperature limit set by organic chemistry. But hydrothermal vents are favourable for life because nutrients are abundant in the heated water.

#### The Young Sun Paradox

There is fossil evidence of life on earth about 3.8 Billion years ago. This leads to the faint young sun paradox. How was liquid water present when the sun was 30% fainter? Probably due to a very strong greenhouse effect, from higher atmospheric carbon dioxide abundance

### Possible environments for light

#### Mars

Mars in in the *habitable zone* around the sun (where liquid water can exist on the surface of a planet). The atmospheric pressure of Mars is also *just* high enough. Imaging and sampling Mars shows that surface water was abundant in the past (over 2Gyrs ago), and radar imaging evidence of liquid water in lakes below the ice caps

Enhanced D/H is evidence of past water that was lost to evapouration (due to Hydrogen and Oxygen being separated, but Deuterium being too heavy to evapourate).

Radar imaging evidence of liquid water in lakes below the ice caps

#### Venus?!

Shocking right? Venus is like an inferno, how the hell can that have life?

Venus has no surface water and an extreme D/H ratio, meaning significant water loss, but there is a possible detection of Phosphine (PH3) in the atmosphere, which might be a *biomarker*. Liquid water drops (in the form of sulfuric acid) do exist on venus, potentially habitable

#### Icy moons of giant planets

Especially Europa, which is resurfaced with liquid water from a tidally heated subsurface ocean. It has a relatively low water content, meaning it has a thin ocean and maybe hydrothermal vents providing nutrients.

Perhaps the deeper oceans on moons with thicker water layers, like Ganymede, will have ice at the bottom because of high pressure, and might lack nutrients.

Enceladus has water geisers with organic chemistry and molecular hydrogen pointing to hydrothermal vents.

Titan may have some form of life in the methane lakes.
