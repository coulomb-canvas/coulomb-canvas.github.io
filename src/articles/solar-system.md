The solar system is made up of many parts. The compenent it's named after is the sun. Now I'm sure your thinking, "The Sun? I've never heard of the sun, what's that?" Go outside during the daytime. Notice that bright glowy thing in the sky? That's what we call the *Sun*.

The sun is a star, like Vega or Alpha Centuri. A good way to tell them apart: take a telescope. Now look at the sun. Notice how you can now only see black? That's because the sun is bright as hell and you're now *blind*. That's a really good way to tell them apart

*Jokes aside, don't look at the sun with a telescope, you will go blind.*

### The Terrestrial Planets

Next we have the four rocky (or terrestrial) planets – Mercury, Venus, Earth (Earth mentioned!!!!) and Mars. These are closer to the sun and are also relatively small compared to some other bodies in the solar system.

They have cores made from metals like Iron and Nickel, and mantles made from *silicates* (which are just rocks basically).

### The Gas Giants

After the rocky planets, we get the four bigger and frankly much cooler *gas planets* – Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune and Uranus (pronounced **Your**-a-nuss, not that there's an alternative, less polite pronunciation anyway, is there?). 

*By the way, since the gas giants are essentially the opposite of the terrestrial planets, I think it's fair for me to describe them as the **extra-terrestrial** planets. Just because the thought of them being aliens is always fun.*

In case you were wondering, they are called gas giants, because they are made of this special material, known as *gas*. For the most part, they are made of these special never-before heard of elements, called hydrogen and helium. Since they are made of gas, they are on average much less dense than the terrestial planets.

But don't let the gas fool you, at their centre they are very much solid. Like the terrestrials, these alien planets do have cores made of iron and nickel (and silicates)

The gas giants also have a LOT of moons.

### Temperatures of Bodies in the Solar System

Like I said, there are no perfect black bodies in the universe, so this next bit is kinda pointless... but what IF bodies in the solar system behaved like black bodies? That would make things a lot easier, right?

<p>I mean think about it, we could then just assume that the rate of energy entering the body is the rate of energy exiting the body:</p> \[ \text{Power in} = \text{Power out} \] \[ \downarrow \] \[ \frac{L_\odot}{4 \pi d^2} \cancel{ \pi R^2 } = 4 \cancel{ \pi R^2 } \sigma T^4 \]

... which would allow you to then rearrange it to get the *equilibrium temperature* of said body:

That's a pretty cool formula, eh? Shame that's only for perfect black bodies tho, so we can't use it. I mean, look at Venus for example. This formula would predict that Venus has a surface temperature of about 57°C, and it in fact has a surface temperature of about 460°C, since it has a super duper thick atmosphere which traps a lot more heat in.

In general, this formula just ignores a LOT of other features of the planets. But for exams, it's usually good enough.
