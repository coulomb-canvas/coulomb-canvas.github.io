For the umptillionth time, we are talking about the sun

The sun is a G2V star. If you remember, the first letter and number basically tell you how hot (and at the same time how *luminous*) it is. G here means it's somewhere in the middle, and the 2 means it's on the hotter end of the 'somewhere in the middle' stars. The V means it's a dwarf star.

The luminosity of the star is described simply by the black-body radiation formula, except you put that weird eyeball by the L and the T, to signify solar luminance and solar temperature. 

<div class=eqn> \[ L_\odot = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T_\odot^4 \] </div>

Not that it'll make much difference, but do remember that our usage of the Stefan-Boltzmann law assumes black-body radiation. 

### Solar activity

#### Convection

If you look at a video of the surface of the sun (aka the *photosphere*) and zoom a fair bit in, you'll see this weird bubbly pattern forming at the top. What the bloody hell is that, you wonder?

This is due to *surface granulation*, and is due to internal convection. See, the brighter bits are where hot gas from the lower layers of the star rises up, because it has more energy, y'know? Then it gets to the top, where it's further away from the nuclear fusion which heats it up, which cools the gas down, hence the darker bits.

#### Sunspots

Speaking of darker bits, if you keep looking on the sun's photosphere, you'll see the occasional black spot. These are areas on the sun which have lower temperatures, of about 3800 K. Now that might not sound low, but compared to the 5800K average, it's pretty low. Clearly, for some bloody reason, the convection isn't allowed to take plave there.

Now you might think "well if these areas are cooler, then shouldn't the hotter gas diffuse into the colder area? 2nd Law of thermodynamics and all that?" Firstly, congratulations of remembering the second law of thermodynamics!! Secondly, hell no.

Remember from the first half of the Quantum and it's Application models, there is something known as the *Zeeman effect*, where you put a particle in a very strong magnetic field, and its degenerate energy levels split into slightly different energy levels. The more splitting there is, the stronger the field becomes. At the sunspots, there is, for whatever reason, a very strong magnetic field, which have a kind of *magnetic pressure* (weird right?) which stops the convection from happening

The global magnetic field is nearly a dipole (meaning it can in most places, it has a clear north pole at the top and a clear south pole at the bottom). But thanks to differential rotation (i.e. the equator rotates faster than the poles), some of the field lines get a bit tangled, with magnetic flux loops emerging at spots and active regions

The magnetic field is generated by a magnetic dynamo. You may remember dynamos from GCSE, in general they convert kinetic energy to magnetic energy. In this case, the sun has things like differential motion and convection due to the conducting plasma, causing the magnetic field to be stronger, (Bulk shearing flow in a conducting medium) which converts kinetic energy to magnetic energy.


#### Above the photosphere

You know how like earth has the statosphere and the thermosphere and all that above its surface? Well the sun has a similar thing. It has the chromosphere, the transition region, and finally the *solar corona*, a very hot, low‑density plasma extending far from the Sun. You can actually see this during an eclipse due to the Thomson scattering of sunlight by free electrons. If you'd like, you can also see it by looking at the X-ray spectra, with optically thin lines.

But there is one problem... why *is* it so hot? Like, it's hotter than the photosphere. Surely the further out you go, the cooler the thingies should get, right?

The main answer is that magnetic loops cause some of the heat from the surface of the sun to come up out of the photosphere, thanks to our good friend the Lorentz force. Since plasma conducts electricity, changing the magnetic field will cause them to move in a particular way.

But what does this mean? Well for one, the solar corona is *structured*, due to magnetic field lines cascading out of active regions, and the *Lorentz force* keeping it in check. In X-rays, we see the extended emission from hot plasma, which suggests heating is related to magnetic fields. The X-rays can erode planetary atmospheres.

#### Helioseismology

Cool as that is, idk maybe it's just me, but the way those bubbles move kinda freaks me out. Makes my skin crawl, not a fan at all. I wonder, if perhaps the inside of the sun if less freaky. But I guess we'll never know, I mean after all, we can't look inside the sun, right?

WRONG!! Turns out, we *can* look at the inside of the sun (well, not look at, but you know what I mean) using a magic trick, known as *helioseismology*, which is the process of the sun blasting dubstep, and us seeing (well, not seeing, but you know what I mean) how the sound waves, particularly *p-waves*, reflect. 

In slightly more professional terms, the sun has a natural oscillation, known as a *p-mode*. If we look at the tiny Doppler shifts and brightess variations this causes, then we can measure the frequencies of the p-modes. From that, we can infer the temperature profile, rotation and composition of the sun, from which we can determine the pressure and density.

We know that, slowly but surely, the sun's luminosity and temperature increase on the main sequence. This leads to something called the young sun paradox, but don't worry 'bout that for now.

### Solar wind

Now we know that magnetic field lines must always be closed at some point, therefore you can never have a truly 'open' magnetic field. However, some field lines are so wide that they are near enough open, which causes the sun to vomit streams of plasma. This is known as *solar wind*, and is the reason comets have ion tails. This can also erode planetary atmospheres. 

At earth's orbit, solar winds have a windspeed of about \\( v = 500 \text{ km s}^-1 \\)

This wind shapes the heliosphere, which is a bubble of plasma in the local interstellar medium

#### Temperature of solar wind

I'm sure everyone is wanting to know this. How *warm* is solar wind? Well we can take an educated guess, by equating kinetic energy with

<div class=eqn> \[ \frac12 m_H v^2 = \frac32 k_BT \] </div>

That give a temperature of about \\( T \sim 1 \times 10^7 \text{ K} \\). Now at first glance you might think "oh damn, that's warm, but what would you expect from the sun itself?" until you stop and realise this temperature is literally comparable to the temperature of the sun itself. What business does plasma have being so hot this far out? Surely it should have cooled off, no?

That must mean that there is an *additional energy source* which actually pushed the solar wind out. It's believe that the wind is assisted by magnetic (*Alfvén*) waves and *magnetic reconnection* in the corona. Magnetic reconnections in coronal loops drives solar flares. The largest events also drive corronal mass ejections (CMEs), which determine space weather at earth and erosion of planet atmospheres.

#### Mass loss rate

With all this vomitting the sun is doing, it must be loosing a lot of weight! Does it need to see a doctor? Should I call 999?

Well, before we check to make sure our friend is healthy, let's do some maths to calculate how much vomitting it's doing. C'mon, the sun has been around for billions of years, it witnessed the dinosaurs, the Qing dynasty, Jesus' death and ressurection, even prime Fortnite, it'll live.

Besides, if anyone should be able to deal with a *corona virus*, it would be the sun, right?

To get the mass loss rate of the sun, consider a thin shell around the sun with radius r and thickness dr. The volume of a tiny strip is the surface area times the width, giving \\( 4πr^2 \ dr \\). The density can be thought of as the number of hydrogen atoms per unit volume, times the mass of a hydrogen atom, giving \\( n_H m_H \\)

<div class=eqn> \[ dm = \rho \ dV = n_H m_H (4πr^2 \ dr) \] </div>

Now if you want the *rate of change* of mass, just divide the whole thing by *dt*. That leaves you with this:

#### Angular Momentum Loss

The plasma being vomitted out is still within the sun's magnetic field, meaning it will also rotate alongside the sun, even at very large radii (known as the *Alfvén radius*). Why is this a problem? Well calculate the angular momentum

<div class=eqn> \[ J = mvr = m \frac{2πr}{P_\text{spin}} r \] </div>

(Oh yeah, angular momentum has rebranded as \\(J\\) for astronomy, to avoid confusion with luminosity). That means:

<div class=eqn> \[ \frac{J}m \propto r^2 \] </div>

Since the arm is so far out (has a large radius), then a very small change in mass can result in a very large change in angular momentum. This is called *magnetic breaking*, which explains why the sun rotates so slowly.
