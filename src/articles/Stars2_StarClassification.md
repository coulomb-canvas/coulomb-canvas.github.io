### Stellar Spectra

If you remember from last time, we had the infamous jumpscare formula, Planck's law:

<div class=eqn> \[ B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2 \pi hc^2 }{ \lambda^5 (e^{\frac{hc}{k_BT\lambda}}-1)}\] </div>

That produced a graph like this. A little bump. We'll call this *the continuum*


As scary as that fromula looks, the continuum is only an approximation. If you look at actual star spectra, you'll notice they look a little more like this, with a few spikes and dips here and there. The intensity spikes are called emission lines and the intensity dips are called absorption lines

#### Emission lines

Emission lines are points where the intensity is higher. Now if the intensity is higher, what do you think would have caused it, hotter of colder gas?

If you picked hotter, you're correct! 

Basically, the high temperature causes electrons to enter higher energy states. Then, when they eventually cool down, they fall into lower energy states, where they release photons, causing the corresponding wavelength of light to increase in its intensity.

These electrons can be excited to any energy level, and they form different series of lines depending on which level they fall back to. If an electron falls all the way back to the ground state, it releases photons with lower wavelengths. These emissions are called *Lyman lines*.

If it falls from a higher state back to the *second* energy level, they form higher wavelength photons which produce the *Balmer lines*. If it falls back to the *third* energy level, they form even higher wavelenght photons which produce the *Paschen lines*.

Remember back to basic Quantum Phenomena, that the energy of a photon is *inversely proportional* to its wavelength:

<div class=eqn> \[ E = \frac{hc}\lambda \] </div>

That therefore means that the electrons which fall all the way back to the ground state have higher energy than those which fall to any other state.

#### Absorption lines

So we know that emission lines form when there is hotter gas. Now think about this, since *absorption* is the opposite of *emission*, take a wild guess as to which kind of gas forms emission lines, hotter or colder?

When you have a gas that's really cold and you put it in front of a source of light, then the colder gas absorbs some of the energy from the source.

To be fair, this probably isn't much different to what you'd expect in everyday life. If you have a light, and put some smoke or something in front of it, you'll see the smoke blocking the light. That's pretty much the same thing which is going on here.

### Classifying Stars

The lines produced from both emission and absorption match the energy transitions of the material in the star. But bare in mind these lines aren't intrinsic properties of the stars, it's an observation which just so happens to work. If a star has hydrogen in it, it *might* produce these lines if the Hydrogen is at the right temperature in the right conditions, so just because there aren't hydrogen lines don't necessarily mean there ain't hydrogen in it.

Either way, people started noticing this convenient little pattern with different spectra and elements and decided to record the different lines different spectra produced.

They then began to classify stars based on their temperature. Unfortunately scientists are pretty stubborn, so they decided to stick with the hydrogen classification naming convention of A-Z, but just reshuffled the letters such that the hotter letters came first and the cooler letters come later, giving us this funky order:

<div class=eqn> \[ O-B-A-F-G-K-M \] </div>

O-stars are hotter, brighter, and are also bluer / whiter. They are also much bigger and much heavier. However, they don't live for very long.

M-stars are colder, dimmer, and redder. They are also smaller and lighter. They do live for a long time, so long that the universe isn't old enough for use to have witnessed the death of an M-star

Everything in the middle gets progressively dimmer, redder and colder as you go from O to M. And ther also go from 0 to 9, 9 being hotter etc. The sun is a G2 star.

Hydrogen can be found in many different ranks, above a certain energy (since they need a sufficient amount of energy to move up a state). Helium is only really found in the O-rank stars, since helium is a noble gas, meaning it's really stable, means it requires a high energy photon in order to go up energy levels. At lower energies, we get molecular lines, since at too high a temperature will cause the molecules to break up.

'Metals' can be found in any star. I put metals in quotation marks, because astronomers have a quite scandelous definition of the word, where a metal is literally anything which isn't hydrogen or helium. At higher temperatures, the metals would be ionised

The size is also classified, where I to IV are giants, V is main sequence, VI is subdwarfs, and D are white dwarfs



### TL;DR

- The absorption / emission lines are dependant on temperature.
- Just because you can't see lines, that don't necessarity mean the corresponding element ain't there.
- The stars are classified as O-B-A-F-G-K-M, where O is hotter and M is colder
